Show an ad only to people who already put something in a cart, or who already looked at pricing twice this week, and the campaign will look like a spectacular performer - not because the ad is unusually persuasive, but because the audience it reached was already, on its own, more likely to buy than anyone else on the internet. That is retargeting's advantage and its measurement problem at the same time. Audience selection alone accounts for most of the apparent return, and a standard platform report has no way to separate that from anything the ad itself actually caused.

There is a direct consequence of this that most vendors avoid saying plainly: without a comparison group that never saw the campaign, a retargeting report cannot be wrong. Every number it produces will look good, whether the ads changed a single outcome or changed nothing at all, because the report is measuring an audience that was already inclined to convert. Frequency-capped, well-sequenced re-engagement clearly does convert some genuine fence-sitters, and that is exactly why the one test that can tell the difference is worth insisting on.

## Why the reported return is almost always good

Every retargeting audience is, by definition, a group that already showed real intent - that is what qualifies someone for it in the first place. A platform's conversion count for that audience will therefore always look strong relative to a cold audience, because the two groups start from completely different baseline odds of converting, independent of anything the campaign itself contributed. Most retargeting reports quietly present that baseline difference as the campaign's own achievement.

This distortion is structural, shared by every platform and every vendor measuring a pre-qualified audience this way, and it comes entirely from how the audience itself was selected. The only way to see past it is to ask what that same audience would have done anyway, which requires deliberately withholding the campaign from a comparable slice of it and watching what happens.

## How it is measured

The number reported is incremental lift: the gap between what the audience that saw the campaign did and what the comparable holdout group did on its own. Budget decisions run off that lift number inside the [attribution layer](/services/marketing-attribution), on the same terms as every other channel's incremental result.

What this channel cannot claim credit for: a purchase from someone who had already decided and was checking out at the moment the ad happened to appear. The holdout exists specifically to keep that share of "conversions" out of the number retargeting gets judged on.

## What we need from you

Whoever manages the ad platform needs room to withhold a comparison segment from every retargeting campaign at setup, since carving out the holdout after a campaign is already running produces a comparison that is no longer clean. Access to the site or CRM events that define who qualifies for an audience in the first place - cart activity, pricing-page visits, trial expiration. And tolerance for a portion of an obviously interested audience seeing nothing for the length of the test, which is the part of this most teams find hardest to accept.
